\noindent \textbf{Abstract}\\
Accurate streamflow simulation and extreme flood risk estimation in large, managed alluvial basins are severely hindered by hydro-climatic non-stationarity, complex hydraulic regulation, and observational scarcity. While machine learning (ML) architectures capture non-linear hydrological interactions, unconstrained algorithms suffer from severe peak-flow attenuation and variance collapse when exposed to out-of-distribution (OOD) hydrometeorological shocks. In this study, we propose the Storage-Adaptive and Regulation-Aware Physics-Guided Multi-Sensor Catchment Hydrology framework (SA-PG-MCH). SA-PG-MCH reconstructs the dynamic hydrological state of managed basins by tracking a continuous latent catchment water storage state ($S_t$) regularized by multi-sensor Earth observations (CHIRPS precipitation, ERA5-Land reanalysis, NASA SMAP root-zone soil moisture, and NASA GRACE/GRACE-FO terrestrial water storage anomalies) and transboundary upstream boundary telemetry. The architecture explicitly separates modeled natural runoff generation from unobserved anthropogenic irrigation abstraction and groundwater recharge fluxes ($U_t = D_t^{\text{irr}} + G_t$). To reduce the tendency of tree ensembles to collapse under extreme events, a state-dependent sigmoidal gating mechanism ($\alpha_t$) dynamically shifts weight to a monotonic physical mass backbone as catchment storage pressure and precipitation anomalies intensify. Calibrated on a 20-year baseline (2000--2019, $N=240$) and evaluated under a strict temporal holdout against the catastrophic 2022 Pakistan Mega-Flood ($N=12$), SA-PG-MCH achieved $\text{KGE} = 0.812$ and restricted peak underestimation bias ($FHV$) to $-4.0\%$, whereas unconstrained GBDT collapsed ($FHV = -13.3\%$). Independent cross-climatic validation on the July 2021 flash flood in the Ahr River Basin, Germany (252 calibration months, 12-month event holdout) confirmed partial structural transferability under zero-shot transfer ($\text{KGE} = 0.421, \text{NSE} = 0.518$), rising to $\text{KGE} = 0.607$ under local calibration. Response surface analysis ($\partial Q / \partial P = f(S, U)$) reveals that runoff sensitivity surges nonlinearly from $0.12$ under dry baseflows to $>0.50$ during saturated states, while human regulation expands the critical saturation threshold $S^*(U)$ from $38.2\text{ mm}$ to $48.5\text{ mm}$, providing up to $+14.8\text{ mm/month}$ of modeled peak flood buffering. Introducing the Flood Amplification Margin ($\text{FAM}_t = S_t - S^*(U_t)$) provides a continuous diagnostic that prospectively predicts observed runoff elasticity ($R^2 = 0.52, p < 0.001$). Closed-loop validation against Sentinel-1 SAR flood inundation extent confirms strong temporal agreement ($r = 0.94, R^2 = 0.88, IoU = 0.79$), while multi-threshold exceedance probabilities provide a potential decision-support indicator for seasonal flood preparedness.\\[1.2em]

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Physics-guided machine learning; Storage-adaptive gating; GRACE terrestrial water storage; Anthropogenic regulation; Response surface; Indus Basin; Sentinel-1 SAR; Out-of-distribution extremes\\[1.5em]

\section{Introduction}

Accurate volumetric streamflow simulation and extreme flood volume estimation in large, managed alluvial basins are critical for transboundary water governance, agricultural security, and flood risk mitigation \citep{adams2024flood, immerzeel2020importance, razavi2022future, young2019pakistan}. The Indus River Basin in South Asia sustains over 230 million people and supports the world's largest contiguous irrigation network---the Indus Basin Irrigation System (IBIS). The lower reach of this basin, encompassing Sindh Province, Pakistan ($140,914\text{ km}^2$), forms a critical hydraulic and socioeconomic bottleneck. Here, the Indus River traverses low-gradient alluvial floodplains (hydraulic slope $<0.8^\circ$) before discharging into the Arabian Sea \citep{hashmi2022hydrodynamic, inam2007geographic}. Because the Lower Indus is heavily regulated by major barrages (Guddu, Sukkur, and Kotri) and canal diversion networks, streamflow is strongly coupled to both upper-basin transboundary inflows and local monsoon forcing \citep{biemans2016future, hossain2025satellite, rodell2018emerging}.

Accelerating climate change has intensified hydro-meteorological extremes across the basin. In July--August 2022, consecutive atmospheric monsoon depressions, coupled with high antecedent saturation and upper-basin snowmelt, delivered torrential rainfall exceeding $350\%$ of normal monsoon climatology across Sindh \citep{nanditha20232022, syed2022analysis, wwa2022pakistan}. Over one-third of the province was submerged under standing floodwaters \citep{tian2023satellite}, displacing 33 million individuals and overwhelming regional storage reservoirs such as Manchar Lake \citep{najibi2018recent, tariq2026retrospective}. Conversely, the basin frequently experiences severe multi-year droughts and critical irrigation deficits \citep{biemans2016future}.

\subsection{Methodological Challenges in Regulated Basin Hydrology}

Simulating monthly streamflow under extreme non-stationarity in heavily managed basins presents fundamental challenges across hydrological modeling paradigms \citep{droppers2024hydrometeorology, mukhopadhyay2024applied, singh2024hydrological}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Limitations of Conceptual Hydrological Models:} Traditional conceptual models (e.g., GR2M, GR4J, SAC-SMA) rely on calibrated bucket stores that assume stationary climate and unmanaged natural flow regimes \citep{frame2022post, perrin2003improvement}. Under unprecedented climatic shocks, static parameterizations break down. Furthermore, conceptual models lack mechanisms to ingest multi-sensor satellite Earth observations (e.g., GRACE terrestrial water storage, SMAP soil moisture, CHIRPS precipitation) without extensive re-calibration.
    \item \textbf{Variance Collapse and Physical Inconsistency in Pure Machine Learning:} Purely data-driven architectures (such as Gradient Boosted Decision Trees, Random Forests, and deep neural networks) excel at mapping non-linear relationships \citep{kratzert2018rainfall, nearing2024global, shen2023differentiable}. However, unconstrained ML algorithms exhibit two critical failure modes during extreme hydrology \citep{beven2020deep, jia2021physics, read2019process}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Violation of Physical Bounding Constraints:} Standard loss functions allow unphysical negative runoff during low-flow periods and unconstrained mass over-predictions.
        \item \emph{Conditional Variance Shrinkage in Decision Tree Ensembles:} Tree ensembles partition feature spaces into orthogonal hyper-rectangles with piecewise-constant predictions. When forced by unprecedented precipitation exceeding historical training maxima, trees produce zero-gradient extrapolations and cap predictions at the mean of the highest historical leaf node. This causes catastrophic peak flow underestimation ($FHV$) during out-of-distribution extremes \citep{grinsztajn2022why, read2019process}.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unresolved Anthropogenic Regulation and Storage Dynamics:} Existing hybrid frameworks often treat human water abstractions and reservoir regulations as unmodeled noise absorbed by residual models \citep{al2023physics, li2026process}. However, in heavily managed alluvial basins, irrigation diversions and groundwater exchanges represent dominant mass fluxes that must be explicitly accounted for to ensure physical consistency \citep{hossain2025satellite, rodell2018emerging, zhang2025hybrid}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Hypotheses and Scientific Objectives}

To resolve these challenges, this study formulates four central hydrological hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 ($H_1$, Nonlinear Storage Transition):} Rainfall-runoff elasticity $E_P = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial P}\frac{P}{Q}$ increases nonlinearly with antecedent catchment storage ($\frac{\partial E_P}{\partial S} > 0$), exhibiting a distinct critical saturation threshold ($S^*$).
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 ($H_2$, Regulation Threshold Expansion):} Anthropogenic canal irrigation withdrawals and groundwater pumping expand the effective catchment storage threshold ($\frac{\partial S^*}{\partial U} > 0$), buffering flood peaks until super-critical storage is reached.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 ($H_3$, Extreme Rarity Scaling):} The peak preservation advantage of state-adaptive physics anchoring expands monotonically with extreme flood rarity ($\frac{\partial \Delta FHV}{\partial T} > 0$).
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 4 ($H_4$, Dimensionless Structural Transferability):} Normalizing critical storage by effective catchment capacity ($S_{\text{norm}}^*(RI) = S^* / S_{\text{capacity}}$) reveals a transferable dimensionless flood response threshold across contrasting macro-alluvial and steep upland basins.
\end{itemize}

\noindent In evaluating these hypotheses, this study delivers four core contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State-Adaptive Physics Arbitration:} An architecture that dynamically shifts weighting ($\alpha_t \to 1.0$) to the mass baseline as storage pressure intensifies, reducing the tendency of tree ensembles to collapse toward historical means.
    \item \textbf{Storage--Regulation Threshold Discovery:} A continuous response law ($S^*(U) = 38.2 + 0.515 U_t$) quantifying critical storage thresholds and supported by empirical historical event validation ($p < 0.001$).
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual Regulation Decomposition:} Attribution of peak flood buffering across irrigation diversion ($+10.6\text{ mm/month}$) and groundwater exchange ($+4.2\text{ mm/month}$) components.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Domain Extreme Generalization:} Robust validation spanning rare-event scaling ($T=2\text{--}20\text{ yr}$), cross-basin transfer spectrums (Pakistan $\to$ Ahr River), synthetic $1.0\times\text{--}5.0\times$ stress testing with Monotonic GBDT controls, and independent post-hoc Sentinel-1 SAR flood extent coupling ($r = 0.94$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Geographical, Hydrological, and Hydraulic Context of the Lower Indus Reach}

The study domain encompasses the Lower Indus Basin alluvial corridor within Sindh Province, Pakistan ($140,914\text{ km}^2$, latitudes $23.4^\circ\text{N}$--$29.0^\circ\text{N}$, longitudes $66.4^\circ\text{E}$--$71.4^\circ\text{E}$, \textbf{Figure 1}). In this study, the Lower Indus reach is operationalized hydrologically as the **Guddu-to-Kotri riverine corridor and downstream delta receiving zone**.

The hydrological boundary conditions are structured as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Upstream Inflow Boundary ($Q_{\text{inflow}}$):} Discharge entering Sindh at Guddu Barrage, integrating runoff from the Upper Indus Basin, snow/glacial melt, and upper riparian tributary inflows.
    \item \textbf{Downstream Discharge ($Q_{\text{obs}}$):} River discharge recorded across the lower barrage chain (Sukkur and Kotri Barrages) discharging toward the Indus Delta and Arabian Sea.
    \item \textbf{In-Basin Catchment Forcing ($P, ET, SM$):} Spatially aggregated meteorological and soil moisture fluxes across the provincial drainage area. While Sindh includes non-contributing hyper-arid desert tracts (Thar) and western ephemeral torrents (Kirthar), the provincial administrative boundary is used because regional flood emergency planning, canal regulation, and barrage operations by the Sindh Irrigation Department and Federal Flood Commission (FFC) are executed at this provincial jurisdictional scale.
\end{enumerate}

Hydraulic control across the province is managed via three major historical barrages: **Guddu Barrage** (inflow boundary), **Sukkur Barrage** (irrigation diversion hub feeding seven major canals), and **Kotri Barrage** (terminal regulatory node upstream of the delta). Regional depression storage is provided by **Manchar Lake**, the largest freshwater lake in Pakistan, which acts as a strategic flood retention basin during severe Indus surges \citep{hashmi2022hydrodynamic}.

\subsection{Observed Catchment Runoff Variable and Invariance to Area Normalization}

Observed streamflow discharge ($Q$, $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$) recorded by the Sindh Irrigation Department and Federal Flood Commission (FFC) barrage telemetry was converted to monthly administratively normalized catchment runoff depth ($Q_{\text{depth}}$, $\text{mm/month}$) via:
\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{depth}} = \frac{Q_{\text{m}^3/\text{s}} \cdot \Delta t}{A_{\text{basin}} \cdot 10^3}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\text{basin}} = 140,914\text{ km}^2$, $\Delta t$ is the monthly duration in seconds, and $10^3$ represents the volumetric conversion factor ($1\text{ mm} \times 1\text{ km}^2 = 1,000\text{ m}^3$). 

\textit{Area Normalization Invariance:} Because the provincial boundary ($140,914\text{ km}^2$) encompasses non-contributing hyper-arid desert tracts (Thar) and western ephemeral torrents (Kirthar), the converted runoff depth $Q_{\text{depth}}$ represents an administrative jurisdictional arealization. If the true hydrologically active contributing area is $A_{\text{eff}} = \eta A_{\text{basin}}$ (where $\eta < 1.0$), both observed and simulated depths scale by the constant scalar factor $c = 1/\eta$:
\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{eff}}(t) = c \cdot Q_{\text{depth}}(t)
\end{equation}
Because dimensionless evaluation metrics (KGE, NSE) and percentage volumetric bias ($FHV$) are strictly scale-invariant under positive scalar multiplication:
\begin{equation}
\text{KGE}(c \mathbf{y}, c \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \text{KGE}(\mathbf{y}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}), \quad \text{NSE}(c \mathbf{y}, c \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \text{NSE}(\mathbf{y}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}), \quad FHV(c \mathbf{y}, c \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = FHV(\mathbf{y}, \hat{\mathbf{y}})
\end{equation}
administrative vs. hydrological area scaling has **zero effect** on model rankings, statistical differences, or percentage volume biases.

\section{Methodology: The SA-PG-MCH Framework}

The proposed **Storage-Adaptive and Regulation-Aware Physics-Guided Multi-Sensor Catchment Hydrology (SA-PG-MCH)** framework couples multi-sensor satellite Earth observations with boundary telemetry through five integrated methodological stages (\textbf{Figure 2}): (1) multi-sensor Earth observation harmonization; (2) dynamic latent storage state formulation with GRACE gravimetry regularization; (3) explicit anthropogenic regulation flux estimation; (4) non-negative physical linear water-balance baseline; and (5) storage-adaptive physics gating, tail-aware multi-objective optimization, and calibrated conformal prediction intervals.

\subsection{Multi-Sensor Earth Observation Harmonization}

The framework ingests a 25-year multi-decadal record (2000--2024, $N=300$ continuous monthly timesteps) spanning six observational dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Meteorological Forcing ($P, ET, T_{\text{2m}}$):} High-resolution gridded precipitation from CHIRPS v2.0 ($0.05^\circ, \sim 5.5\text{ km}$), alongside potential evaporation and 2-meter air temperature from ERA5-Land reanalysis ($0.10^\circ, \sim 10\text{ km}$).
    \item \textbf{Terrestrial Moisture States ($SM, \text{TWSA}$):} Volumetric root-zone soil moisture ($0\text{--}100\text{ cm}$) from NASA SMAP L4 / ERA5-Land ($9\text{ km}$), combined with monthly Terrestrial Water Storage Anomalies ($\text{TWSA}$, mm equivalent water thickness) from NASA GRACE/GRACE-FO JPL RL06 Mascon solutions ($0.25^\circ$).
    \item \textbf{Hydraulic Boundary Telemetry ($Q_{\text{inflow}}$):} Gauged transboundary discharge records at Guddu Barrage, representing upper Indus inflows entering Sindh.
\end{enumerate}

Observed streamflow discharge ($Q, \text{m}^3/\text{s}$) is converted to area-normalized monthly runoff depth ($Q_{\text{depth}}, \text{mm/month}$) to ensure spatial scale invariance:
\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{depth}}(t) = \frac{Q_{\text{m}^3/\text{s}}(t) \cdot \Delta t}{A_{\text{basin}} \cdot 10^3}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ is the monthly duration in seconds, and $A_{\text{basin}}$ is the contributing basin area ($140,914\text{ km}^2$ for the Lower Indus; $746\text{ km}^2$ for the Ahr Basin at Altenahr).

\subsection{Dynamic Latent Water Storage State Formulation and Causal Updating}

Classical lumped hybrid models rely on the empirical Antecedent Precipitation Index ($API_t$), which acts as an ad-hoc heuristic rather than a physical storage reservoir. SA-PG-MCH replaces $API$ with a dynamic **modeled water-balance storage state** $S_t$ constrained by satellite gravimetry. To eliminate circular feedback, the framework computes storage and streamflow sequentially:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prior Storage Update ($S_t^-$):} Before predicting streamflow at month $t$, the prior water storage is accumulated from antecedent storage, precipitation, and upstream inflow minus evaporative demand and regulation abstractions:
    \begin{equation}
    S_t^- = \max\left(0, \; S_{t-1} + P(t) + Q_{\text{inflow}}(t) - ET(t) - \hat{U}(t)\right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{State Gating and Streamflow Prediction ($\hat{Q}_t$):} The prior storage $S_t^-$ determines the standardized storage anomaly $\text{SPI}_t$, driving the gating operator $\alpha_t$ and composite prediction $\hat{Q}_{\text{composite}}(t)$ (Eqs. 6--8).
    \item \textbf{Posterior State Correction ($S_t$):} The final posterior storage state is updated by subtracting simulated discharge and assimilating satellite gravimetry observations:
    \begin{equation}
    S_t = \max\left(0, \; S_t^- - \hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t)\right) + K_t \left(\text{TWSA}_t - \mathcal{H}(S_t^-)\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $K_t$ is the Kalman-style gravimetry assimilation gain ($K_t = 0$ when gravimetry is unavailable or masked), and $\mathcal{H}(\cdot)$ is the standardized observation operator.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{SA-PG-MCH Dynamic State Estimation and Streamflow Inference}
\label{alg:sapgmch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Monthly inputs $\mathbf{X}_t = \{P_t, ET_t, T_{2\text{m},t}, SM_t, Q_{\text{in},t}, \text{TWSA}_t\}$, previous state $S_{t-1}$, parameters $\{\boldsymbol{\beta}, \mathbf{a}, \kappa_1, \kappa_2, \delta_0\}$.
\ENSURE Simulated streamflow $\hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t)$, 95\% intervals $[\hat{Q}_{\text{lower}}(t), \hat{Q}_{\text{upper}}(t)]$, updated storage $S_t$.
\STATE Estimate human regulation flux: $\hat{U}_t \leftarrow \kappa_1 Q_{\text{in},t} + \kappa_2 \max(0, ET_t - P_t)$
\STATE Compute prior storage state: $S_t^- \leftarrow \max(0, S_{t-1} + P_t + Q_{\text{in},t} - ET_t - \hat{U}_t)$
\STATE Compute standardized anomalies: $\text{SPI}_t \leftarrow \frac{S_t^- - \mu_S}{\sigma_S}, \; \text{FA}_t \leftarrow \frac{P_t - \mu_P}{\sigma_P}, \; \text{IA}_t \leftarrow \frac{Q_{\text{in},t} - \mu_{\text{in}}}{\sigma_{\text{in}}}$
\STATE Compute monotonic physical baseline: $Q_{\text{phys}}(t) \leftarrow \beta_0 + \beta_P P_t + \beta_S S_t^- + \beta_{\text{in}} Q_{\text{in},t}$
\STATE Compute state-adaptive physics gate: $\alpha_t \leftarrow \sigma(a_0 + a_1 \text{SPI}_t + a_2 \text{FA}_t + a_3 \text{IA}_t)$
\STATE Predict non-linear residual: $\hat{R}_t \leftarrow \text{GBDT}(\mathbf{X}_t, S_t^-, \hat{U}_t)$
\STATE Blend composite discharge: $\hat{Q}_{\text{composite}}(t) \leftarrow \alpha_t Q_{\text{phys}}(t) + (1 - \alpha_t)(Q_{\text{phys}}(t) + \hat{R}_t)$
\STATE Apply mass envelope cap: $W_{\text{total}}(t) \leftarrow P_t + Q_{\text{in},t} + S_t^-$
\STATE Enforce bounds: $\hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t) \leftarrow \max(0, \min(\hat{Q}_{\text{composite}}(t), W_{\text{total}}(t)))$
\STATE Conformal interval bounds: $\delta_t \leftarrow \delta_0(1 + 0.85\max(0,\text{SPI}_t) + 0.65\max(0,\text{FA}_t))$
\STATE $[\hat{Q}_{\text{lower}}(t), \hat{Q}_{\text{upper}}(t)] \leftarrow [\max(0, \hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t) - \delta_t), \min(W_{\text{total}}(t), \hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t) + \delta_t)]$
\STATE Update posterior storage: $S_t \leftarrow \max(0, S_t^- - \hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t)) + K_t(\text{TWSA}_t - \mathcal{H}(S_t^-))$
\RETURN $\hat{Q}_{\text{final}}(t), [\hat{Q}_{\text{lower}}(t), \hat{Q}_{\text{upper}}(t)], S_t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{equation}
\hat{Q}_{\text{lower}}(t) = \max\left(0, \; \hat{Q}(t) - \delta_t\right), \quad \hat{Q}_{\text{upper}}(t) = \min\left(W_{\text{total}}(t), \; \hat{Q}(t) + \delta_t\right)
\end{equation}
where $\delta_t = \delta_0 \cdot (1 + 0.85 \max(0, \text{SPI}_t) + 0.65 \max(0, \text{FA}_t))$ expands uncertainty dynamically under extreme storage pressure.

\subsection{Storage-Regulation Runoff Response Surface and Flood Amplification Threshold Law}

To understand how antecedent catchment storage and human regulation jointly govern extreme flood generation, we formulate the rainfall-runoff elasticity $E_P = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial P} \frac{P}{Q} = f(S_t, U_t)$ and map the resulting continuous response surface (\textbf{Figure 4}):

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Figure_Storage_Regulation_Response_Surface.png}
\caption{Storage-Regulation Runoff Response and Threshold Law: (a) 2D Flood Amplification Diagram mapping rainfall elasticity $E_P$ across storage anomaly $\text{SPI}_t$ and regulation intensity $RI = U_t / (P_t + Q_{\text{in},t})$, with critical threshold $E_{\text{crit}} = 0.50$; (b) Continuous critical storage threshold law $S^*(U) = 38.2 + 0.515 U_t$ across discrete regulation intensities; (c) 4-stage counterfactual regulation decomposition for the August 2022 Mega-Flood ($C_0$: Factual, $C_1$: No Irrigation, $C_2$: No Groundwater, $C_3$: Modeled Naturalized).}
\label{fig:response_surface}
\end{figure}

As shown in \textbf{Figure 4a--b}, the catchment exhibits a distinct threshold transition:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Statistical Formulation and Bootstrap Confidence Intervals of $S^*(U)$:} Defining the critical storage threshold as $S^*(U) = \{S \mid E_P(S, U) \ge 0.50\}$, non-parametric bootstrap regression ($B=1,000$) on the calibration baseline yields:
    \begin{equation}
    S^*(U) = 38.2 \; [35.6, \; 40.8] + 0.515 \; [0.442, \; 0.588] \cdot U_t \quad (\text{mm})
    \end{equation}
    Comparing candidate parametric formulations confirms that the linear expansion model ($\text{AIC} = 14.2, R^2 = 0.992$) is statistically superior to quadratic ($\text{AIC} = 16.1, p_{\text{quad}} = 0.68$) and exponential saturation models ($\text{AIC} = 15.8$). Evaluating $S^*(U)$ across a discrete regulation grid ($U = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25\text{ mm/month}$) reveals that active irrigation diversions expand the critical retention threshold from $38.2\text{ mm}$ (natural flow) to $51.1\text{ mm}$ ($U=25\text{ mm/month}$), delaying the onset of super-critical runoff amplification.
    \item \textbf{Flood Amplification Margin ($\text{FAM}_t$) and Out-of-Sample Predictor Comparison:} We define the continuous **Flood Amplification Margin** as the storage distance to critical threshold crossing:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{FAM}_t = S_t - S^*(U_t) \quad (\text{mm})
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{FAM}_t < 0$ denotes a sub-critical absorbing state and $\text{FAM}_t > 0$ denotes super-critical flood amplification. When evaluated prospectively on the unseen 2016--2024 test period ($N=108$ months, with $S^*(U)$ fixed exclusively on 2000--2015), 1D $\text{FAM}_t$ quantitatively predicts observed monsoon rainfall conversion efficiency ($R^2 = 0.524, \text{RMSE} = 0.179, p = 9.38 \times 10^{-5}$), outperforming 1D Precipitation Anomaly $\text{FA}_t$ ($R^2 = 0.432, \text{RMSE} = 0.195$) and 1D Storage Anomaly $\text{SPI}_t$ ($R^2 = 0.287, \text{RMSE} = 0.219$). Events with $\text{FAM}_t < 0$ exhibited suppressed conversion ($E_{\text{obs}} = 0.17 \pm 0.05$), while events with $\text{FAM}_t \ge 0$ surged to $E_{\text{obs}} = 0.52 \pm 0.09$ (prospective KS test: $D_{\text{KS}} = 0.667, p = 3.97 \times 10^{-3}$; permutation test: $p_{\text{perm}} < 0.0001$).
    \item \textbf{Latent State Grounding with Independent Earth Observations:} To verify that latent storage $S_t$ tracks real terrestrial hydrology, model states were compared against independent satellite observations. Simulated $S_t$ exhibits strong agreement with NASA GRACE/GRACE-FO TWSA ($r = 0.748, p = 1.18 \times 10^{-14}$) and NASA SMAP root-zone soil moisture ($r = 0.941, p = 3.50 \times 10^{-36}$), confirming that $S_t$ accurately represents physical catchment moisture dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Physical Derivation of $S_{\text{capacity}}$ and Dimensionless Threshold Law ($S_{\text{norm}}^*(RI)$):} Catchment storage capacity was determined from physical soil root-zone properties ($S_{\text{capacity}} = \theta_{\text{eff}} \cdot Z_{\text{root}}$) using FAO/ISRIC SoilGrids and European Soil Data Centre databases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Lower Indus Alluvial Plain:} Effective root depth $Z_{\text{root}} \approx 1.2\text{ m}$, available water capacity $\theta_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.11\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$, giving $S_{\text{capacity}} = 85.0 \pm 6.2\text{ mm}$.
        \item \emph{Ahr River Upland Basin:} Shallow rocky cambisols ($Z_{\text{root}} \approx 0.45\text{ m}, \theta_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.09\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$), giving $S_{\text{capacity}} = 40.0 \pm 4.5\text{ mm}$.
    \end{itemize}
    Defining regulation intensity $RI = U_t / (P_t + Q_{\text{in},t})$, the dimensionless threshold law becomes:
    \begin{equation}
    S_{\text{norm}}^*(RI) = \frac{S^*(U)}{S_{\text{capacity}}} = 0.449 \; [0.418, \; 0.480] + 0.606 \; [0.520, \; 0.692] \cdot RI
    \end{equation}
    When the Ahr headwater reach is evaluated with negligible modeled regulation ($RI \approx 0$), the dimensionless critical threshold aligns between the macro-alluvial Lower Indus ($S_{\text{norm, Indus}}^* = 0.449$) and steep upland Ahr Basin ($S_{\text{norm, Ahr}}^* = 0.425$, $\Delta = 0.024$), indicating that relative catchment wetness at $\sim 43\text{--}45\%$ of total effective capacity governs the onset of nonlinear runoff amplification across diverse climate regimes.
    \item \textbf{4-Stage Counterfactual Regulation Decomposition ($C_0\text{--}C_3$):} During the August 2022 Mega-Flood ($P = 166.4\text{ mm}, Q_{\text{in}} = 126.4\text{ mm}$, \textbf{Figure 4c}):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{$C_0$ (Observed Factual Flow):} Simulated discharge equals $132.8\text{ mm/month}$.
        \item \emph{$C_1$ (No Irrigation Diversions, $D_t^{\text{irr}} = 0$):} Peak discharge rises to $143.4\text{ mm/month}$ ($\Delta Q^{\text{irr}} = +10.6\text{ mm/month}$).
        \item \emph{$C_2$ (No Groundwater Exchange, $G_t = 0$):} Peak discharge rises to $137.0\text{ mm/month}$ ($\Delta Q^{\text{GW}} = +4.2\text{ mm/month}$).
        \item \emph{$C_3$ (Modeled Naturalized Basin, $U_t = 0$):} Peak discharge reaches $147.6\text{ mm/month}$ ($\Delta Q^{\text{human}} = +14.8\text{ mm/month}$).
    \end{itemize}
    The counterfactual model estimates that canal irrigation diversions contributed the primary flood buffering effect ($\sim 72\%$), supplemented by regional groundwater storage ($\sim 28\%$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Rare-Event Return Period Scaling, Cross-Basin Transfer Spectrum, and Sentinel-1 Validation}

To evaluate how model performance scales with extreme flood rarity, we quantify the peak volume preservation advantage $A_T$ across return periods $T \in [2, 20]\text{ years}$ fitted via Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution on annual peak series:
\begin{equation}
A_T = \big|FHV_{\text{GBDT}}(T)\big| - \big|FHV_{\text{SA-PG-MCH}}(T)\big|
\end{equation}
Connecting $\text{FAM}_t$ directly to flood rarity demonstrates that rarer floods correspond to progressively deeper super-critical threshold excursions: $T=2\text{ yr} \implies \text{FAM} = -8.4\text{ mm}$ ($A_{T=2} = +1.7\%$), $T=5\text{ yr} \implies \text{FAM} = +2.1\text{ mm}$ ($A_{T=5} = +5.3\%$), $T=10\text{ yr} \implies \text{FAM} = +9.8\text{ mm}$ ($A_{T=10} = +7.6\%$), and $T=20\text{ yr} \implies \text{FAM} = +16.7\text{ mm}$ ($A_{T=20} = +9.3\%$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Figure_Sentinel1_Flood_Inundation_Validation.png}
\caption{Rare-event scaling, transferability spectrum, and independent Sentinel-1 validation: (a) Peak volume bias $|FHV|$ across return periods $T = 2\text{ to }20\text{ years}$, showing expanding physics-anchoring advantage $A_T$; (b) Cross-basin transferability spectrum from Pakistan to Ahr River (Zero-Shot $\to$ Few-Shot $\to$ Local Calibration); (c) Independent post-hoc Sentinel-1 SAR inundation extent validation ($r = 0.94, R^2 = 0.88, \text{RMSE}_{\text{area}} = 2,410\text{ km}^2, \text{MAE}_{\text{area}} = 1,780\text{ km}^2$); (d) Static ($\alpha=0.50, \alpha=c^*=0.62$) vs. dynamic state-adaptive ($\alpha_t$) physics gating benchmark.}
\label{fig:sentinel1_validation}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expanding Physics-Anchoring Advantage with Flood Rarity:} As shown in \textbf{Figure 5a}, for moderate high-flow events ($T = 2\text{ yr}$), the advantage of SA-PG-MCH over unconstrained GBDT is modest ($A_{T=2} = +1.7\% \; [0.8\%, 2.6\%]$). However, as flood rarity intensifies ($T = 5\text{ yr} \to 10\text{ yr} \to 20\text{ yr}$), the peak attenuation of pure ML worsens dramatically (from $-4.8\%$ to $-13.3\%$), while SA-PG-MCH remains strictly constrained ($-3.1\%$ to $-4.0\%$). Consequently, the physics-anchoring advantage expands monotonically to $A_{T=20} = +9.3\% \; [6.8\%, 11.8\%]$ during the 20-year 2022 Mega-Flood, corroborating recent findings on extreme-event physics anchoring \citep{nearing2024global, shen2023differentiable}.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Basin Geographic Transferability Spectrum (Pakistan $\to$ Ahr River):} When transferring the model trained exclusively on the Lower Indus to the steep Ahr River Basin in Germany (\textbf{Figure 5b}):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Zero-Shot Transfer (no local parameter fitting):} Retains positive hydrological skill ($\text{KGE} = 0.421, \text{NSE} = 0.518, FHV = -24.8\%$), providing evidence of partial structural transferability across contrasting geomorphic regimes.
        \item \emph{Few-Shot Adaptation (12 months of local calibration):} Performance improves to $\text{KGE} = 0.548, \text{NSE} = 0.635, FHV = -19.4\%$.
        \item \emph{Full Local Calibration (2000--2020):} Achieves $\text{KGE} = 0.607, \text{NSE} = 0.702, FHV = -17.5\%$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent Post-Hoc Sentinel-1 SAR Validation:} \textit{Sentinel-1 SAR observations were used strictly for independent post hoc physical impact validation and were excluded from model parameter calibration.} As illustrated in \textbf{Figure 5c}, simulated monthly discharge exhibits strong temporal agreement with satellite SAR flood extent ($r = 0.94, R^2 = 0.88, IoU = 0.79$) with moderate spatial area error ($\text{RMSE}_{\text{area}} = 2,410\text{ km}^2, \text{MAE}_{\text{area}} = 1,780\text{ km}^2$).
    \item \textbf{Value of Dynamic State Adaptivity vs. Static Gating:} As shown in \textbf{Figure 5d}, fixed equal gating ($\alpha = 0.50$) causes peak flood overestimation ($FHV = +4.2\%$), while static cross-validated optimal gating ($\alpha = c^* = 0.62$) produces peak underestimation ($FHV = -7.1\%$). In contrast, dynamic state-adaptive gating ($\alpha_t$) achieves optimal balance ($\text{KGE} = 0.812, FHV = -4.0\%$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Research Directions}

While the SA-PG-MCH framework demonstrates strong physical consistency and predictive fidelity across both basins, several limitations should be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Basin Geomorphic Trade-offs:} In the steep, unmanaged Ahr River catchment, conceptual GR2M achieved lower peak volume bias ($FHV = -4.8\%$) than SA-PG-MCH ($FHV = -17.5\%$), although SA-PG-MCH outperformed all purely data-driven ML baselines. This highlights that in rapid upland flash-flood catchments, lumped conceptual storage buckets retain strong diagnostic value.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Resolution Constraints:} The current framework operates at a monthly timestep, which is optimized for basin-wide volumetric water balance and strategic seasonal reservoir planning. However, resolving sub-daily hydrographs in ephemeral western hill torrents (Kirthar Range) requires sub-daily (hourly) hydro-meteorological models.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Watershed Delineation:} This study operationalized the Lower Indus Basin using the provincial administrative boundary ($140,914\text{ km}^2$) to align directly with provincial flood emergency operations. Converted runoff depth $Q_{\text{depth}}$ is an administrative jurisdictional arealization. Future research will couple the state-estimation system with 2D hydrodynamic inundation models (e.g., LISFLOOD-FP, HEC-RAS 2D) and explore large-sample global benchmark datasets (e.g., CAMELS, Caravan).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}

The principal contribution of this study is the formulation of a **state-adaptive physics-guided learning paradigm** that uses remotely constrained storage and unresolved human regulation to discover when a river basin transitions from rainfall absorption to rapid flood amplification. Evaluated across the 25-year Lower Indus Basin record (2000--2024, $N=300$) and independently tested on the catastrophic July 2021 Ahr River flash flood in Germany (252 calibration months, 12-month event holdout), the main scientific conclusions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State-Adaptive Physics Arbitration Reduces Variance Collapse:} By dynamically shifting weight to the monotonic physical mass backbone ($\alpha_t \to 1.0$) as catchment storage pressure and precipitation anomalies intensify, SA-PG-MCH restricted peak flow underestimation to $-4.0\%\ [-8.2\%, +1.5\%]$ during the 2022 Pakistan Mega-Flood, reducing the tendency of tree ensembles to collapse toward historical training means.
    \item \textbf{Storage--Regulation Runoff Response Surface and Threshold Law:} The non-linear elasticity $E_P = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial P}\frac{P}{Q} = f(S_t, U_t)$ reveals a sharp regime transition where precipitation sensitivity surges from $0.16$ under dry conditions to $>0.50$ during saturated states. Non-parametric bootstrap regression establishes the critical storage threshold law $S^*(U) = 38.2 \; [35.6, 40.8] + 0.515 \; [0.442, 0.588] \cdot U_t\text{ mm}$, supported by independent empirical historical event testing ($p < 0.001$). Counterfactual analysis estimates that agricultural regulation reduced simulated downstream peak volume by up to $14.8\text{ mm/month}$ during the 2022 Mega-Flood.
    \item \textbf{Monotonic Growth of Physics Advantage with Flood Rarity:} Evaluating peak volume preservation across return periods $T = 2\text{ to }20\text{ years}$ demonstrated that the advantage of state-adaptive physics anchoring expands monotonically from $+1.7\%$ during 2-year events to $+9.3\%$ during the 20-year 2022 Mega-Flood, directly supporting recent extreme-event physics-guided modeling paradigms.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Loop Satellite Radar Flood Inundation Validation:} Coupling discharge simulations directly with multi-temporal Sentinel-1 SAR flood inundation extent mapping ($A_{\text{flood},t}$) confirms strong landscape-scale physical coupling ($r = 0.94, R^2 = 0.88, \text{RMSE}_{\text{area}} = 2,410\text{ km}^2, \text{MAE}_{\text{area}} = 1,780\text{ km}^2, IoU = 0.79$).
    \item \textbf{Extrapolation and Cross-Basin Transferability:} Synthetic stress testing demonstrated that under $1.0\times\text{--}5.0\times$ cloudburst forcing, ordinary and monotonic GBDT plateau into piecewise-constant leaf cuts, whereas SA-PG-MCH scales along the continuous mass gradient. Furthermore, zero-shot transfer to the Ahr River Basin retained positive predictive skill ($\text{KGE} = 0.421, \text{NSE} = 0.518$), providing evidence of structural transferability.
\end{enumerate}

The proposed hybrid modeling methodology provides an accurate, computationally efficient, and physically consistent hydroinformatics tool for climate-resilient water management and flood risk mitigation in large, data-scarce transboundary river basins.
